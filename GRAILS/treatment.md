# GRAILS
### A Treatment

**Written by:** [Author]
**Genre:** Heist Thriller / Dark Comedy
**Tone:** *Uncut Gems* meets *Game Night* with the soul of *Desk Set*
**Logline:** A humble sports memorabilia restorer fights for the soul of his craft when he's swept up into a Koreatown gang's heist of an influencer's multimillion-dollar collection.

---

## ACT ONE

The movie opens in motion. A seventeen-year-old at a Saturday garage sale in Reseda, squinting in the white-hot sun at a cardboard box half-buried under a folding table. He pays four dollars. His face is blank. He has no idea what he's holding. By the time he's back in his car, the TikTok is already up — shaky hands, bad lighting, a mumbled caption: *found this lol* — and the notifications are erupting before he's pulled out of the driveway.

Kevin sees the post. Kevin DMs. Kevin drives forty minutes in traffic, pays cash, and is on the 101 north before the kid has refreshed his phone. The box moves through Los Angeles like contraband, each pair of hands holding it a beat longer than necessary, something ancient and wordless pressing up through the cardboard. Kevin winds up in the Valley, in a strip mall that looks like a crime scene from 1994, and sets the box on the counter of ZZ Heritage House.

Zach picks it up. Everything slows.

ZACH ZAKALOWICZ — sun-faded 1984 Durham Bulls tee, jeweler's loupe hanging from a leather cord around his neck — holds the 1986 Fleer unopened box set the way a surgeon holds a newborn. The montage, which has been kinetic and sardonic up to this moment, simply stops. The shop smells of acid-free plastic and aged cardboard and the chalky ghost of forty-year-old bubblegum dust. Behind Zach: walls of graded slabs, binders of sets, a framed Dominican Sugar Kings pennant, a pair of scuffed Jordans suspended in a vitrine like they're still mid-flight. Kevin shifts in the chair in the back. Checks his phone. Checks again. The most important transaction of his life is being held up because Zach has a family at the counter.

Three generations: a grandfather in a windbreaker, a father with tired eyes, a ten-year-old boy watching everything. Shoeboxes of the grandfather's hoard. Zach works through them with surgical honesty — a Bill Ripken error card, a Randy Johnson Marlboro ad, a lot worth maybe five hundred dollars, hope deflating in the father's face. Then Zach reaches under the counter and produces a slabbed 1989 Upper Deck Ken Griffey Jr. Rookie. Crisp. Glowing. Certified 9. He turns it over and shows the holographic "Restored" notation on the back. He logged it himself. He won't pass off restoration as factory original — not at any price, not for any amount. The father's eyes mist. He pulls his own father into a hug while the ten-year-old watches, suddenly understanding that adults cry over cardboard because time itself is inside the cardboard, and it's running out.

Zach walks them to the door. He's the last warm light in a dying strip mall. To his left, a RadioShack sign hangs by a single wire. To his right, a Spirit Halloween banner peels off a gutted department store. The parking lot is cracked asphalt and weeds. There's a rent notice on his counter. He is a solitary monk guarding a temple that the world has already forgotten to visit.

Kevin wants the box appraised. He says it's a gift for his brother — perfect restoration, just like new, $10,000 upfront. Zach examines the contents with a loupe and a penlight and his own trembling hands: the Michael Jordan rookie, the Larry Bird–Magic Johnson XRC, the Patrick Ewing. A Jordan and a Kobe in an unopened wax box from 1986. The collector world treats items like this as holy relics. Zach tells Kevin it's real, it's extraordinary, and it's too much money. Kevin smiles and tells him there's a man who wants to see him. Zach doesn't yet know what he's just agreed to.

Word travels fast in Koreatown. A man Zach has never met makes a phone call. The job is real.

JAE-WOOK is a lieutenant in one of K-Town's sovereign organizations — the kind that runs its own economy, its own justice, its own calendar. He is not who the movies told you he would be. He is principled in the way that requires hard choices, noble in the way that leaves marks. He never hurts anyone who doesn't deserve it. He only steals from collectors who don't care about the lore. When Zach tries to ask questions, he is politely, firmly told not to. This is Koreatown. The infrastructure is invisible. The rules are older than the city.

The rooftop meeting happens at dusk, soju poured, the city orange and smog-beautiful below them. Jae-Wook lays out the number: one hundred thousand dollars. Zach has two months of rent left. He has a restoration in his back room that is the greatest single work of his craft. The math is not complicated. In the background, barely registered, a young woman named DEJA sits with her phone and her swallowed smile, logging everything. Zach says yes.

The next morning, the shop door opens and ELIZABETH walks in. They call her Zab. She is the world's foremost AI authentication expert — sophisticated, precise, carrying the quiet weariness of someone who traded her soul for a stability that became a gilded cage. She and Zach were engaged. They both carry the same jeweler's loupe, matching, from the early days of the shop. She still has hers hidden in her pocket. She's come, she says, about some business. What she means is she came to see if it was still here. All of it — the shop, the smell, the version of her that existed inside these walls. It is. She is with Togg now. Zach watches her leave.

Jae-Wook explains the full scope. He explains GREGG TOGG.

Togg is what happens when a man with no object permanence becomes the most followed sports memorabilia collector in the world. He is high-frequency, hyper-saturated, a walking YouTube thumbnail — his 8K god-ray lighting follows him into every room like a deity's favor, and he believes it does. He treats everything as a collectible: the items, the crowd, the people around him. His penthouse in the Hills is not a home; it is a Panopticon of prestige, everything for sale, everything a backdrop. He uses the word "authenticity" the way a company uses it in a tagline — as marketing, not belief. He's about to launch $GOAT, a sports memorabilia memecoin, and he plans to use a multimillion-dollar verified collection as the anchor. The 1986 Fleer box — the Jordan, the Kobe — is the crown jewel. The item he doesn't have. Yet.

What Jae-Wook wants Zach to build is a perfect forgery of that box. What the crew will do is swap it in, let the coin launch, let the markets go stratospheric, and short the whole thing into the ground on the way down. Zach now knows exactly what he's building and why.

The long con begins. HYE-JIN, Jae-Wook's cousin — rave scene, OnlyFans, hiding in the most visible possible place — moves through a Koreatown karaoke party and makes Togg's retinue feel like they discovered her. She is the empath of the crew, dangerously good at being where people need someone to be.

---

## ACT TWO

Procurement is a montage of obsession. Zach sources the materials the way a jeweler sources diamonds — through networks, through favors, through knowing exactly what questions to ask and who will lie to you if you ask them the wrong way. Period-correct wax stock. Correct-era cardboard. Pigment that matches a 1986 press run to within a spectrophotometer's margin of error. He handles everything with the care of a man who has spent his life believing that the object is sacred, even when — especially when — it is not real.

Zach and Elizabeth cross paths at a Togg event space in the Hills without knowing it. He is there under the cover of a procurement errand. She is there because she works there now, moving through the god-ray lighting with practiced ease while her loupe sits against her hip in her pocket like a compass she no longer trusts herself to follow. They see each other across a room of overlit collectibles. Neither knows what the other is doing there. The conversation is short. The charge between them is not.

Jae-Wook takes Zach to a private dinner — white tablecloths, Saudi representatives, a fence with a Geneva accent and careful eyes. The 1986 Fleer box, if authenticated, will move to a private buyer in the Gulf for a sum that has multiple commas. The stakes are no longer abstract. Zach sits with this information in the Uber home, the city scrolling past the window, and feels the familiar vertiginous feeling of being past the last exit.

Togg goes live. He announces $GOAT to millions of simultaneous viewers — a sports memorabilia memecoin backed by his verified collection, anchored by the crown jewel he has "just acquired" (he has not yet acquired it; he is Togg; the announcement is the acquisition). The market is primed. The prediction markets are already moving.

Deja — who has been laughing at Togg's jokes for weeks, who has been running the full intelligence operation while appearing to be merely charming — coordinates the short positions with the precision of someone who was, in another life, on track for a business degree at USC until AI made that life impossible. She swallows her smile. She undercuts. Hye-Jin, Deja, and Jae-Wook take their positions. The trap is set.

Zach works. Night after night in the amber glow of ZZ Heritage House, the forgery taking shape with the same obsessive precision he has given to every authentic restoration in his career. This is the revelation the film has been building toward: a man whose entire identity rests on the sanctity of authentic things discovers that he is, constitutionally, a natural at manufacturing it. His hands know how. His eye knows why. The line between preservation and fabrication is thinner than he thought. He is not sure what that means about him.

Jae-Wook comes to the shop. He sees Zach bent over the work, and something in the precision of it troubles him. He asks if Zach understands what he's crossing. Zach looks up. He says yes. He crosses it anyway. The line is visible, and he steps over it, and the film understands that this is the moment his arc resolves — not a fall, exactly, but a transformation. The man who lived by the truth is learning to manufacture it with the same love and care.

Togg announces Elizabeth on livestream — VP of Authenticity, his prestige asset, the world's foremost AI authenticator endorsing his collection. Zach watches from the shop, alone, the god-ray lighting making her look like someone he no longer knows. She smiles for the camera. He sees the loupe pocket. He turns off the screen.

A car has been parked outside the shop for three days. Zach notices the same pair of eyes twice on different streets. He is being followed by police. The work is nearly done. The clock is visible.

Then the shop is gone. Liquidation — the landlord exercises an option, the rent arrears, a technicality that has always been there waiting to be used. Zach stands in the parking lot in the dying light of the San Fernando Valley with everything he owns in boxes and a forgery in a padded case and nowhere left to be honest. He is past the point of no return. He was past it when he said yes on the rooftop. He just now understands it.

The crew assembles. Final coordination in a Koreatown backroom — Zach, Jae-Wook, Deja, Hye-Jin. Everyone knows their role. The board is set. The box is ready.

---

## ACT THREE

The event is everything Togg has promised and more: a livestreamed collector's showcase in his Hills Panopticon, eight-figure items under 8K lighting, millions of viewers watching the crown jewel's unveiling. The 1986 Fleer box. The swap has already happened. The box on Togg's white display table is the one Zach built, night after night, with love and precision and grief and craft. It is indistinguishable from the real thing — not despite Zach's code, but because of it.

Elizabeth runs the authentication. This is what she was hired to do. She positions the AI rig over the box — the system she built, the parameters she wrote, the training data she curated over years working alongside a man in a sun-faded tee in a dying strip mall. She reads the outputs. The room is silent. The crew watches on monitors from a parking structure across the canyon. Hye-Jin has made herself scarce after weeks in Togg's orbit — Deja had to pull her back. *Some people you have to let life fix.* Hye-Jin heard it. Deja's smile stayed swallowed.

The Jordan card authenticates. Stratospheric. $GOAT hits its peak in the time it takes the crowd to exhale. Prediction markets resolve. The short triggers. The positions pay.

Elizabeth stares at the output. Her AI has confirmed the authentication. And then she sees it.

It is small. It is not an error — it is a signature. A ghost in the data, the kind of thing that would only register to the person who trained the AI, because it's the same tell she taught it to look for in Zach's early work: a fractional inconsistency in the pressure gradient of the aging simulation, a thing she once called his "habit" — affectionately, years ago, standing in the shop with her loupe. It is not a mistake. It cannot be a mistake. It is Zach saying, in the only language they still share: *I was here.*

A beat of silence. Elizabeth's face shows nothing. She knows. He knows she knows. Everyone else in the room and the millions watching do not.

$GOAT collapses in real time. Togg, live on camera, does not understand what is happening as the numbers invert — the exposure surfaces, the provenance unravels, the authentication that was supposed to sanctify his empire quietly eats through the foundation. The crash is instantaneous, public, and total. He is, at his peak, exactly what he has always been: a man who believed authenticity was a marketing term. The market agrees. The downfall was always built into the worldview.

The aftermath is quiet. Zach, Jae-Wook, Deja, and Hye-Jin move through it the way you move through the hour after a long night — careful, a little unreal. The short positions have paid. Jae-Wook is exact in his accounting. Everyone walks away with exactly what was agreed. No more, no less. The shop is gone. The family collection is scattered. But Zach has money, and money is specific, and he knows where his father's collection went.

He buys it back at auction — not all of it, but the things that carry the weight. The Griffey he cannot buy back because he gave it to that family. He doesn't want to. Some things you let go because they found where they belong.

He walks out of the auction house into Los Angeles light. He puts his hand in his pocket. The loupe is there — the one Elizabeth gave him, the one that matches hers, the shared object that has been in every pocket and every scene, the visual heartbeat of the film. He turns it in his fingers.

Elizabeth is there. She has been there for a moment. They don't explain themselves. They've said everything that can be said, and some things you say with objects instead of words. She reaches into her pocket and produces her loupe — the same one, the pair. She holds it out. He holds his up. Two identical instruments for measuring the truth of things, worn down to the same patina, surviving the same years.

What the film asks is not whether they get back together. What it asks is whether you can be the person you were before the world priced you — and whether the things you love are still there, in the dark, waiting to be held the right way.

*GRAILS* is a movie about authentication in an era that has automated it. About the soul of craft in an economy that has commodified the soul. The influencer economy didn't happen to us — we funded it, followed it, and let it set the price on things we said we loved. Zach and his crew don't take that economy down. They just refuse to let it be the last word.

The loupe catches the light. The frame holds on it.

*FADE OUT.*
